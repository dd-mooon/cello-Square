Logistics Terms ULD (Unit Load Device)

등록일 2025-03-26

Unit load devices (ULDs) are pallets and containers used to load freight onto aircraft and optimize cargo capacity. Proper ULD loading is crucial for airlines and freight forwarders to maximize space utilization, reduce costs, and ensure load stability. By standardizing cargo sizes and shapes, ULDs facilitate the rapid loading and unloading of airplanes, significantly reducing turnaround times and enhancing operational efficiency. This in-depth blog post explores ULD optimization for air freight, including key terminology, their types, processes and benefits.

Key Takeaways
  • A ULD (Unit Load Device) is a standardized container or pallet designed to fit aircraft belly holds, consolidating individual shipments into a single unit for rapid, secure loading.
  • ULDs fall into two categories — pallets and containers — with IATA setting the global specification and handling standards (ULDR).
  • A ULD ID code consists of a 3-letter type/size prefix, a 4–5 digit serial number, and a 2-character owner code (e.g., AKN 12345 DL).
  • Core benefits: optimized hold space, load stability, shorter ground turnaround, and reduced fuel burn and carbon emissions.

1. Types of Unit Load Devices and Their Identification Codes

ULDs are ingeniously designed to conform to the specific dimensions and contours of various aircraft, maximizing the efficiency of space utilization and facilitating swift loading and unloading processes. This customization is crucial for optimizing cargo distribution within the aircraft's hold, enhancing flight safety and operational efficiency. The International Air Transport Association (IATA) plays a pivotal role in setting the global standards for ULD specifications and handling. By establishing technical specifications, IATA ensures a uniform approach to the design, operation, and safety of ULDs across the international air transport industry.

ULDs can be broadly categorized into two types: pallets (or air pallets) and containers.

Types of Unit Load Devices

1) Pallets

These flat metal platforms, typically made of lightweight yet sturdy aluminium, are used for stacking and securing cargo. Goods are fastened onto these pallets using nets or meshes, which are then adapted to fit the unique contours of an aircraft's hold. This adaptability allows for the most efficient use of available space, ensuring that every inch of the cargo hold is utilized effectively.

2) Containers

Similar to their sea-faring counterparts, air freight containers come in a myriad of shapes and sizes to accommodate the diverse requirements of different aircraft. These containers are designed with specific purposes in mind, such as refrigeration for perishable goods, lateral openings for easy access, airtight conditions for sensitive cargo, or ventilation for live transports.

(Source: Getty Images)

It's also important to note that the dimensions, weight limits, and usage regulations of ULDs vary according to each airline's specific guidelines. These parameters are meticulously outlined in the airline's terms and conditions, ensuring that all ULDs are used within their operational and safety limits. Understanding these guidelines is essential for logistics professionals and shippers to ensure compliance and secure the safe transportation of goods across the skies.

ULD Identification Codes

The identification system for Unit Load Devices (ULDs) is a meticulously designed framework that ensures the traceability and efficient management of these essential components in air freight operations. As outlined, each ULD is marked with a distinctive ULD number that provides essential information about its type, characteristics, unique identification, and ownership. Here's a closer look at each segment of the ULD number and what it signifies:

1) Three-letter Prefix

This segment indicates the ULD's type and key characteristics. For example, "AKN" specifies that the ULD is a forkliftable LD3 container. Each combination of letters is standardized to represent different types of ULDs, allowing for immediate recognition of the ULD's functionality and compatibility with aircraft.

2) Serial Number

Following the prefix, a 4 or 5 digit serial number uniquely identifies the ULD. The length of the serial number depends on when the ULD was manufactured or put into service. If it was before October 1, 1993, it would have a 4-digit serial number. For ULDs entering service after this date, the serial number can be either 4 or 5 digits long. This numerical sequence ensures that each ULD can be distinctly identified from others of the same type.

3) Two-character Suffix

The ULD number ends with a two-character suffix that identifies the owner of the ULD. In many cases, especially for airline-owned ULDs, this suffix matches the IATA designator codes for the airline, such as "DL" for Delta Air Lines in the provided example. This part of the ULD number is crucial for establishing ownership and responsibility for the maintenance and handling of the ULD.

For example, "AKN 12345 DL" tells us that the ULD is a forkliftable LD3 container (AKN), it has a unique identifier of 12345, and it is owned by Delta Air Lines (DL). This systematic approach to ULD identification streamlines logistics operations, enabling efficient tracking, handling, and utilization of ULDs across the global air freight network.

2. What are the benefits of ULDs?

The significance of Unit Load Devices (ULDs) in the air cargo transportation industry cannot be overstated. These specialized containers and pallets are not just about moving goods; they represent a sophisticated system designed to enhance efficiency, safety, and environmental sustainability. Let's delve deeper into the ways ULDs contribute to the industry:

1) Optimal Space Utilization

ULDs are designed with standardized sizes and shapes, fitting perfectly into the cargo holds of aircraft. This standardization ensures that the available space is used to its maximum potential, allowing more cargo to be transported in a single flight. By optimizing the space, airlines can carry more goods, increasing revenue opportunities and reducing the number of flights needed to transport the same amount of cargo.

2) Ensuring Safety

The design of ULDs facilitates secure packing methods, which are crucial for the safety of the cargo and the aircraft. Properly secured cargo minimizes the risk of movement or shifting during flight, which can affect the aircraft's balance and stability. This securement is vital for preventing damage to the cargo itself and ensuring the safety of the flight.

3) Streamlining Processes

The global standard identification system for ULDs, with its unique alphanumeric codes, has revolutionized tracking and handling processes. This system simplifies the logistics of transferring ULDs between airlines and airports, ensuring that cargo can be tracked in real-time and handled efficiently worldwide. Such standardization has significantly reduced the potential for errors and delays in the cargo handling process.

4) Compatibility & Efficiency

The classification and identification through ULD codes not only inform about the type and characteristics of the ULD but also about its compatibility with different aircraft models. This information helps in planning and loading, ensuring that cargo is transported in the most suitable and efficient manner. It streamlines operations, reducing the time and labor involved in loading and unloading, and contributes to faster turnaround times for aircraft.

5) Environmental Benefit

The space-saving nature of ULDs has a direct impact on environmental sustainability. By maximizing the cargo space, airlines can optimize their fuel consumption. This efficiency translates into fewer emissions per kilogram of cargo transported, contributing to the aviation industry's efforts to reduce its carbon footprint. In an era where environmental concerns are paramount, the role of ULDs in promoting more sustainable air transport practices is increasingly valuable.

In summary, ULDs are a cornerstone of the air cargo industry, offering a blend of operational efficiency, safety, and environmental benefits. Their role extends beyond mere containers for cargo; they are integral to the modern, efficient, and sustainable movement of goods across the globe.

3. ULD Handling Process

The utilization of Unit Load Devices (ULDs) in air cargo operations showcases a highly organized and efficient process, essential for maintaining safety, balance, and optimal performance during flights. The journey of cargo from ground to air involves several critical steps, each contributing to the seamless transport of goods across the globe. Let's break down these steps further:

1) Cargo Build-Up

  • The process begins with the strategic loading of cargo onto pallets or into containers, following a carefully planned load distribution. Heavier and bulkier items are placed at the bottom to serve as a stable base, while lighter and more fragile items are positioned on top. This careful arrangement ensures the cargo's balance and stability, which is crucial for the aircraft's safe operation.
  • Once the cargo is arranged, it is secured to the pallet or within the container using straps, nets, or other restraining systems. This step is vital to prevent any movement of the cargo during transit, which could lead to imbalances or damage to the cargo and the aircraft.

2) Loading Cargo

  • Ground handling teams employ specialized equipment, including forklifts and main deck loaders, to carefully load ULDs into the aircraft. This equipment is designed to handle the size and weight of ULDs efficiently, ensuring their safe and precise placement into the cargo compartment.
  • The placement of ULDs inside the aircraft is governed by a specific loading plan. This plan accounts for the aircraft's design, ensuring optimal weight distribution and adherence to balance requirements. Proper positioning is critical to maintain the aircraft's center of gravity within safe operational limits.

3) Securing Cargo

  • Once loaded, ULDs are secured in place using the aircraft's Cargo Loading System (CLS). This system may include additional straps, nets, or locking mechanisms designed to keep the ULDs stationary during flight. By effectively becoming part of the aircraft's structure, ULDs contribute to the overall integrity and safety of the flight.

4) Aircraft Compatibility

  • ULDs are not one-size-fits-all; they are designed to fit specific types of aircraft. This specialization ensures that each ULD maximizes the available space within the cargo compartments of particular aircraft models. Choosing the correct ULDs for an aircraft type is crucial for efficient space utilization and safe cargo transportation.

5) Transshipment

  • In a hub-and-spoke system, ULDs may need to be transshipped to reach their final destination. This process involves transferring ULDs from one aircraft to another, often at a central hub airport. Transshipment is a critical component of global logistics networks, allowing for the consolidation of cargo from various origins to be efficiently distributed to their respective destinations.
  • By enabling the quick transfer of ULDs between flights, airlines can significantly reduce aircraft turnaround times. This efficiency is crucial for maintaining tight schedules and ensuring timely deliveries, a key factor in the competitiveness of air cargo services.

6) Return Journey

  • After delivering their cargo, ULDs may return empty to their origin or be filled with goods for the return journey. This cyclical process ensures the continuous flow of goods across global networks and maximizes the utilization of ULDs.
  • The decision to send ULDs back filled or empty often depends on the balance of trade and cargo availability. In routes where cargo flows are imbalanced, ULDs might return empty more frequently, presenting challenges in logistics efficiency and cost.

7) Tracking and Management

  • The integration of GPS, RFID, and IoT technologies into ULD management systems has revolutionized how these assets are tracked and managed. These technologies provide real-time data on the location, condition, and status of ULDs, enhancing the security and efficiency of cargo transportation.
  • Real-time tracking enables airlines and logistics providers to optimize their operations, reduce losses and damages, and respond promptly to any issues that arise. This level of visibility is also a key factor in meeting the increasing demand for transparency and reliability in the supply chain.

8) Freight Cost Calculation

  • Airfreight costs can be calculated based on the pivot weight of the ULD. This method considers the weight at which the cost of shipping a ULD becomes more economical than shipping the cargo loose. It encourages the efficient packing of ULDs, optimizing space utilization and fuel consumption.
  • By using the pivot weight system for cost calculation, the air cargo industry promotes standardized handling and billing practices. This approach simplifies the pricing structure, making it more transparent and equitable for shippers.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. Does the shipper need to provide the ULD?

ULDs are owned and managed by airlines or specialist ULD leasing and management companies; forwarders and ground handlers may handle them operationally. Shippers do not normally provide their own ULD — though non-conforming packaging can trigger on-site rework costs.

Q2. What do ULD codes like 'AKE' and 'PMC' mean?

AKE denotes an LD3-class lower-deck container; PMC denotes a 96×125 inch large pallet. The first three characters are a standard code for type, size, and aircraft compatibility.

Q3. What is pivot weight?

It is the base weight included in a ULD's minimum charge. Weight above the pivot is billed at an over-pivot rate, so compare total cost against bulk rates.

Q4. How are ULDs tracked?

Airlines and forwarders use ULD management systems plus RFID/IoT-enabled smart ULDs to track location and status. Shippers typically track at shipment level using the AWB number.

Q5. How does a ULD differ from an ocean container?

Ocean containers are ISO-standard steel boxes used across ship, truck, and rail. ULDs are lightweight units shaped to aircraft hold contours — differing in dimensions, materials, ownership, and rate structures.
